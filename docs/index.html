01
WHAT IT IS

A bridge, not a translator

VoiceMate is a classroom assistant for schools where the teacher is comfortable in Hindi and the students are most comfortable in Santhali. The teacher keeps teaching the way they always have. The class hears the lesson in Santhali, answers back in Santhali, and the teacher gets that answer in Hindi.

Underneath there are four moving parts: speech into text, translation between the two languages, a model that rewrites things at the right level for the grade, and text back into speech. Around that we've wrapped lesson material in both languages, worksheets, flashcards, a record of what happened in class, and an offline layer, because in most of these schools the network drops in and out all day.

Nothing goes in front of a class unseen. The teacher can open any translation, fix the wording, and only then hand it to the students.

Small print, since this is a prototype

The translation you can click through today runs on a hand-written phrasebook of classroom lines, not a trained model. Speech recognition and speech output lean on the browser where it's available. Everything here is the shape of the thing; the production ASR, NMT and TTS still have to be dropped in, and a native Santhali educator has to sign off on the content before it ever reaches a real classroom.

02
THE PROBLEM

Both sides are doing their best

The teacher in a tribal-area school is often trained entirely in Hindi. The students in front of them speak Santhali at home and have probably heard very little Hindi outside of it. Nobody in that room is failing, and the lesson still lands badly.

  • Concepts get explained once, in the wrong language, and half the room nods without following
  • When students answer, the teacher has to guess what they actually said
  • Interactive work, group activities, quick checks, all of it slows to a crawl
  • There's nobody in the building to write the local-language material
  • Mother-tongue teaching stays a nice idea that can't be scaled across schools
  • And the cloud apps that could help assume a connection most of these classrooms don't have
03
THE LESSON LOOP

One sentence, both ways

The whole product is a loop that runs in both directions. Going out: the teacher's Hindi becomes Santhali the students can hear. Coming back: a student's Santhali becomes Hindi the teacher can act on.

The pedagogical step in the middle matters more than it looks. Instead of translating a sentence word for word, it takes the grade, subject, topic and learning objective into account and produces something a Class 3 student can actually use.

05
GOING OFFLINE

Assume the signal dies

This is the part we care about most. Lessons and language data get pulled down while the connection is good, kept on the device, and used regardless of what the network does during the school day. Anything that happens in class is written locally first and pushed up when the internet comes back.

STEP 01 Cache

Lessons and language data come down while the network is still there.

STEP 02 Store

They stay on the teacher's device, not behind a login on a server.

STEP 03 Teach

The class keeps running with the connection switched off.

STEP 04 Sync

Whatever happened in class is waiting to go up when the signal returns.

THE STACK

What it's built with

Deliberately boring choices. The app has to run on the low-cost phones teachers already carry, and the backend has to survive a school with no reliable connection.

Frontend

Flutter (Dart) One Android app that behaves on cheap hardware.
Material Design Plain, predictable screens a teacher can learn in a minute.

Backend

Python + FastAPI AI services, content management and sync endpoints.
REST APIs How the app and the backend talk to each other.

AI & ML

ASR Speech from teacher or student into text.
Translation Hindi ↔ Santhali, both directions.
Pedagogical AI Explanations, activities and questions pitched at the right grade.
TTS Translated text back into something the room can hear.

Offline intelligence

On-device models Enough of the core loop to keep going with no signal.
SQLite / local storage Lessons, language data and cached content on the phone.

Database & sync

PostgreSQL Users, curriculum and learning data in one place.
Sync manager Pushes local changes up whenever connectivity returns.

Cloud & delivery

Google Cloud Backend and central services.
GitHub Where the code and the history live.

11
PAYOFF

What this buys the classroom

  • Learning in the mother tongue Students understand the idea first and the language never gets in the way.
  • Hindi-medium teachers can teach No retraining, no learning Santhali before being useful.
  • Real two-way conversation Questions go out in Santhali and answers come back in Hindi.
  • Content that fits the class Same topic, pitched at whatever grade and objective was chosen.
  • It survives no signal The lesson doesn't stop because the tower is down.
  • Printable bilingual material Lessons, worksheets and flashcards in Hindi and Santhali together.
  • Room to grow The same architecture can carry Ho, Mundari and whatever comes next.
12
IMPACT

Where we expect to land

The point is to shrink the gap between teacher and student in schools where mother-tongue teaching usually dies on logistics. Santhali is the first language because that's the problem statement we were given, but nothing in the design is specific to it.

Translation was never the product

Plenty of tools translate a sentence. What a classroom needs is the translation wrapped in the right difficulty level, offline behaviour, material you can hand out, and a teacher who signs off before anything is said out loud.

That combination is what we're building. Santhali first, and the rest of the tribal languages after.

Honest about where it stands

Right now this is a working prototype of the flows, not a deployed classroom product. The phrasebook is small and hand-checked, the AI models are stubs waiting for real ones, and every piece of generated content needs a native Santhali educator to review it before students see it.
